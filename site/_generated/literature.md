## 2. What the literature confirms

**The ticket system broke a basic flow rule.** Raineri & Earl state that ticketing should be separated from admissions and that the flow between them should be sequential, one-directional and free of crossing conflicts (2005, p. 213). In 2026 a buyer stood in line at the ticket window and then walked to a second line at the exchange counter, and both lines extended into the floor between the stage crowd and the bar (density map, `density_2026_vs_plan_en.png`). The HSE location factors listed by Shao et al. include "route is blocked by lines of people" (2019, p. 950). The plan follows the same rule with one processing step and one line per serving station. The step count alone does not explain the 2026 lines: payment at the station with the 2026 head count still leaves a long wait (technical report, "The plan against the alternatives"), because too few people served beer.

**Processing rate is a crowd-safety variable.** In Fruin's FIST model, "Time" covers event scheduling and facility processing rates (Raineri & Earl 2005, p. 209), and time-based strategies aim to keep flow below the capacity of every element of the venue (p. 213). From 18:00 on, the ticket window and the exchange counter both received more buyers than they could serve. Their lines occupied the floor, so the shortfall affected safety as well as sales.

**A changeover surge is the expected pattern.** Raineri & Earl report that crowds migrate during a festival to reach facilities and food and drink stalls (2005, p. 210). Johansson et al. describe crowd flow as an inverse-parabola function of density, with maximum throughput at intermediate density, so high throughput is hard to sustain (2012, p. 153). The event study in this study (`09_time_series_models.png`) shows lines building within 10 to 20 minutes after each band ends.

**People choosing their line rationally is a defensible assumption.** The model has attendees pick the line with the shortest expected wait and skip lines longer than their patience. Karayaman & Yıldız report that current crowd psychology rejects the picture of crowds as irrational: from a social identity perspective, behaviour follows shared norms and perceived legitimacy (2026, p. 6, citing Stott & Adang 2018 and Borch 2013).

**An agent-based model fits a crowd of this size.** Sharma et al. point out that the right model depends on the setting: agent-based models are less suited to 100,000-person gatherings, and flow models handle complex layouts poorly (2018, p. 488, citing Still 2007). With 2,000 attendees in a single hall, agent-based simulation is within the range where it is appropriate.

**The study's capacity concern matches static-capacity practice.** Sharma et al. cite Still's rule for static areas: occupant capacity equals area in m² divided by 0.5, that is 2 people/m² (2018, p. 487). The model's free standing floor is 708 m² (stage, bar zone, vendor strips and tables excluded), which gives 1,416 people by that rule. For 2,000 tickets the model puts about 1,530 people inside at the fullest moment, of whom about 1,340 stand on the free floor (1.9 people/m², `capacity.csv`). Vendor strips and the table area add 346 m² at lower density. The 2026 attendance was therefore close to the static limit of the floor. The model reaches the NFPA 101 limit for assembly areas (2.2 people/m²) near 2,300 tickets, or between 1,900 and 2,800 tickets for a hall 10% smaller or larger per side.

## 3. New inferences from existing outputs

**Faster service raises heavy drinking, and the model does not limit it.** Across the 48 scenario nights, the share of attendees with 6 or more beers rises from 4.6% in the 2026 setup to 19.6% under the plan (range across nights 10.8% to 29.5%), and the share with 8 or more from 1.1% to 9.4% (4.1% to 17.2%). Beers per drinker go from 2.2 to 4.0. The only limit in the model is one beer per 14 minutes. The sources treat this as a known risk:

- Alcohol was the most common reason for arrest in the surveyed stadiums: 22 of 46 reported reasons, or 48% (Ammon & Fried 1999, pp. 137–139).
- Stadiums that did not sell alcohol had fewer arrests (p. 141).
- Organizers should set a purchase limit, refuse service to intoxicated patrons, place security where alcohol is sold, and never make alcohol the event's primary source of income (Abbott & Abbott 2000, p. 109, citing Miller 1997).
- Festival attendees and staff named intoxication and drugs among the main risks (Raineri & Earl 2005, pp. 207–208).

The sales figure in the report (+2,908 beers per night, about MXN 232,600 at the assumed price) should be presented together with service limits and is no sales target.

**Front-of-stage density is capped by an assumption.** The model stops people from choosing a cell above 3.0 people/m². The front-of-stage density it reports (median peak 2.8 to 3.0 people/m² up to 2,500 tickets, `output/runs/staffing_grid.csv`) is therefore partly an input. The thresholds in the literature sit above that cap: Still places unstable movement at 4 people/m² (cited in Shao et al. 2019, p. 951), and NFPA data place walking speed near zero at 3.77 people/m² (p. 950). At 3,000 tickets the cap can no longer hold and the model reports a median of 4.9 people/m² in front of the stage (3.0 to 6.8 across nights). The current model can say that more than about 2,500 tickets pushes the front past 4 people/m². It cannot say how dense the front was at 2,000.

**Evacuation has not been checked.** Shao et al. simulated a 672 m² music venue, close to this hall's 708 m² of free floor. Evacuation finished within the 8-minute Green Guide benchmark at 1 to 2 people/m² but not at 3 to 4 people/m², and the number of open exits dominated the result at higher densities (2019, pp. 955–957). The Orus Fest model has a single assumed entrance and never evacuates the hall.

## 5. Strategies for the organizer that the sources support

These extend the report's recommendations. They are operational and do not need new simulation.

1. **One-step sales with service limits.** Take payment at the serving station, set a limit of drinks per purchase, train bar staff to refuse visibly intoxicated patrons, and place security near the bar (Abbott & Abbott 2000, p. 109; Ammon & Fried 1999, pp. 123–124).
2. **Barriered, one-directional lines that do not cross the floor.** Use barriers to form orderly lines toward the bar wall and keep ticketing or payment separate from the stage-to-bar movement path (Struniawski 2024, p. 31; Raineri & Earl 2005, p. 213).
3. **Keep exits and the routes to them clear of lines and sales points.** Lines and sales points along evacuation routes are listed risk factors (Shao et al. 2019, p. 950; Karayaman & Yıldız 2026, p. 10).
4. **A crowd management point with sight of the floor.** Raineri & Earl recommend a central crowd management and communications centre with a view of the venue and cameras on blind spots and pressure points (2005, p. 211). Struniawski describes real-time video for estimating density and deciding on waiting areas (2024, p. 32).
5. **Signage and announcements.** Clear signs for bar locations, line direction, payment method and alcohol policy (Abbott & Abbott 2000, pp. 107–108; Ammon & Fried 1999, p. 126). Announce changeover times and bar status between sets (Karayaman & Yıldız 2026, p. 13).
6. **Trained staff and briefing.** Casual staff often receive limited instruction. Training should cover crowd movement, incident handling, communications and conduct that avoids triggering dangerous behaviour, with a pocket reference guide (Raineri & Earl 2005, pp. 211–212; Ammon & Fried 1999, pp. 141–142; Petrica 2024, p. 41).
7. **Pre-event coordination.** Meet with police, fire service and local authorities a few days before the event (Struniawski 2024, p. 32; Petrica 2024, p. 41).
8. **Performers as part of crowd management.** Briefing performers on behaviour that moves the crowd forward (Raineri & Earl 2005, p. 212) matters most during Heppner and Solitary Experiments, when the front is densest.
9. **Ticket cap tied to floor space.** Static capacity at 0.5 m² per person (Still, cited in Sharma et al. 2018, p. 487) and the NFPA 101 limit put the free floor at about 1,400 to 1,550 people at once. That corresponds to about 2,100 to 2,300 tickets with the attendance pattern in this study, and the hall size carries a margin of 10% per side.
10. **Document and review.** Liability follows foreseeability. A problem that has already happened is foreseeable and calls for due care (Ammon & Fried 1999, pp. 123–124). The 2026 lines are now documented, so the next edition is held to that standard. Keep a written crowd management plan and a post-event review (Abbott & Abbott 2000, p. 108; Petrica 2024, p. 41).

## 6. Limits of the sources

Most sources address stadiums, large outdoor festivals, protests or mass gatherings of 10,000 people or more. Only Raineri & Earl (2005) and Shao et al. (2019) deal with music events, and only Shao et al. model a venue of comparable size. None quantifies bar queues at an indoor concert. Their density thresholds come from different authors and settings (Still, NFPA, FEMA, Fruin; Shao et al. 2019, pp. 950–951) and are reference ranges; none was set for this hall. Ammon & Fried's arrest data are from 1990s US stadiums with 28 respondents.
